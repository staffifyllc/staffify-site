A useful first step, before a hiring decision

Illustrative example. These are assumed numbers, not a client result or a promise of time saved.

Suppose you coordinate 20 deliveries a week and spend 10 minutes per delivery chasing files and revisions. That is 3 hours and 20 minutes a week. This task alone would not justify a full-time hire. Start with a clearer handoff, then measure what still needs you.

One checklist for each jobCorrect property and job; all scoped files present; filenames and formats match; delivery link opens with the intended access; unresolved issues are listed.
A clear split of responsibilityA coordinator checks the objective requirements and tracks revisions. You keep taste decisions, scope changes and final approval.
An explicit stop ruleMissing requirements, uncertain quality, new scope or a deadline at risk go back to you. Nobody quietly approves an exception.
Try it before expanding the roleUse the checklist on five authorized past jobs. Record missed items, escalations and your review time. Decide what is actually worth handing over.

The part that does not scale

Most owners I talk to have already solved the shooting and most of the editing. What they have not solved is the last step: someone has to look at the file before it goes to the agent, decide whether it meets the standard, and push it back with notes when it does not. That someone is usually the owner.

Hiring an editor does not remove that step. It moves it. You stop editing and start reviewing, chasing revisions, and answering the client while the revision is in flight. On a slow week that is fine. On a heavy week it is the thing that decides how late you work.

There are good reasons to run it that way. You may be the only person who knows what a finished file looks like for your brand, and handing that over badly costs more than doing it yourself. Whether it is still worth your evenings is a question only you can answer, and the answer may well be yes.

What a second person actually owns

This is the division that matters, and it is the one most staffing pitches leave vague. You keep the standard. They keep the traffic.

The VA owns

  • Intake: files in, named, filed, logged against the job
  • First-pass check against your written checklist
  • Sending revisions to the editor and tracking them to done
  • Delivery links, galleries, and the delivery email
  • Client questions about status, timing, and re-sends
  • Scheduling and calendar traffic
  • Flagging anything outside the checklist and stopping

You keep

  • What the standard is, in writing
  • Final approval on anything the checklist does not cover
  • Taste calls: grade, pacing, what a hero shot is
  • The client relationship and anything commercial
  • Editor selection and feedback that changes how they work
  • Anything a mistake would cost you the account

Specialist editing or true quality control on the craft itself is a different role with a different skill assessment. If that is what you actually need, say so and I will scope that instead, or tell you we are not the right fit.

How the handoff and review work

You write the checklist onceTen to twenty lines. What a finished file looks like for your brand, in your words. I help you write it. It is yours either way.
They check against it, and only against itPass, fail, or unsure. Unsure comes to you. They never quietly decide a taste call on your behalf.
Fails go back to the editor with the line they missedThe note references the checklist, so the same correction does not get relitigated every project.
You move to sampling when you decide toStart by reviewing every file. Once the work has shown you it holds up, and only when you are ready, move to reviewing a sample at whatever rate suits you. You set that rate, and you can put it back to everything at any time.
Escalation is a rule, not a judgement callNew client, new property type, anything the checklist does not name, anything a client pushed back on before. Those stop and come to you.
If your quality standard only exists in your head, this is the real work. Writing it down is most of the value, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever hire anyone. That is why the role map below is free and does not require a call.

When I would tell you not to hire

SituationWhat I would say
Under about 10 hours a week of coordinationA written checklist and a shared folder structure will get you most of the way. Do that first and see what is left.
Work is seasonal and thin between peaksPart-time or nothing. A full-time seat you cannot feed turns into the thing you manage rather than the thing that helps.
Your standard is genuinely undefinableIf the call really is pure taste every time, it stays with you, and there may be nothing here worth delegating.
