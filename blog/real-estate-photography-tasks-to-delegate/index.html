15 Things Real Estate Photography Agency Owners Can Hand Off First

The small jobs between shoots quietly turn a photography agency owner into the person every project passes through. Here are 15 to hand off first, with what done looks like and what to keep.

Staffify Team · September 22, 2026 · 8 min read

The shoot is finished. The project isn't.

Photos need a final review. A video needs a revision. The agent wants the listing website. A floor plan is still missing. Someone needs to tell the client what is coming next.

For a real estate photography agency owner, these small jobs can fill the space between every shoot, sales conversation and evening at home. Each one seems manageable. Together, they turn the owner into the person every project has to pass through.

At Flylisted, we put these responsibilities into a project manager's job description: overseeing client deliverables from the day after the shoot until the project is closed. This guide adapts that role into 15 practical handoffs you can consider in your own agency.

You do not need to delegate all 15, buy new software or hire a full-time person to start. Pick the work that repeats, has a clear finish line and keeps pulling you away from the work only you can do.

1. Keeping the master shoot list current

Hand off: Updating each job with its promised deliverables, deadlines, assigned people and current status. Your project manager should be able to tell you what is due today, what is waiting on someone and what is at risk.

Done looks like: One current record for each shoot, with an owner and a next action for every open item.

You keep: Decisions about priorities when deadlines conflict. The person maintaining the list should flag the conflict before it becomes a missed promise.

2. Tracking every deliverable after the shoot

Hand off: Checking that the photos, video, floor plan, tour and other purchased assets are accounted for. A job is not complete because the photos came back if the client also ordered a video.

Done looks like: Every item in the order is marked received, under review, awaiting a correction or ready to deliver.

You keep: Decisions about changes to scope. A missing item and an extra request are different problems.

3. Running the morning photo review

Hand off: A first review against your written standards: correct property, complete set, requested edits, consistent presentation and anything obviously missing or incorrect. Give the reviewer examples of work you would approve and reject.

Done looks like: A reviewed set with specific issues marked for correction, rather than an owner opening every folder to discover what happened.

You keep: Creative judgment and final approval until the reviewer has demonstrated that they understand your standard. Coordination and expert image review are different skills.

4. Requesting corrections and checking that they were made

Hand off: Turning review notes into precise editing requests, sending them to the right person and checking the returned version. Include the image or file, the issue, the required change and the deadline.

Done looks like: Each request has an answer, and the revised file has been checked against the original note.

You keep: Disputed standards, unusual edits and changes that affect what the client purchased.

5. Coordinating the video editing queue

Hand off: Keeping editors supplied with the brief, assets, format requirements and revision notes. Your project manager can chase a missing version and confirm the next delivery time without you becoming the messenger.

Done looks like: Everyone knows which version is current, what is outstanding and when the next version is expected.

You keep: Creative direction and approval of exceptions. Managing a video editor does not mean the project manager must also be the video editor.

6. Building single-property websites

Hand off: Assembling the property page in your existing platform with the approved address, agent information and media. Our project manager role description uses RelaHQ; the handoff can work in the platform your agency already uses.

Done looks like: The page has been checked for the correct property details, working media and links, and the intended agent branding before release.

You keep: Template choices and unusual client requirements. Make the repeatable build a documented process.

7. Tracking listing-site domain renewals

Hand off: Maintaining a list of domains approaching expiration and asking the client whether the site should stay live, using your approved renewal process.

Done looks like: A recorded renewal decision before the expiration date, with any unresolved decision escalated.

You keep: Pricing exceptions and authority to incur charges. Tracking a renewal does not authorize someone to purchase it without the agreed approval.

8. Preparing YouTube thumbnails

Hand off: Creating thumbnails from an approved template and the correct property assets. A clear example is more useful than an instruction to "make it look good."

Done looks like: A readable thumbnail with accurate details, the right image and consistent agency branding.

You keep: The visual direction. If original design is required, assign it to someone with that skill rather than assuming it comes with an administrative role.

9. Preparing and updating YouTube videos

Hand off: Loading the approved video, title, description, thumbnail and links, then checking the intended visibility and version before publication.

Done looks like: The correct video is ready in the correct account, with accurate property information and a working destination link.

You keep: The publishing approval until the process is established, plus decisions about channel strategy and unusual content.

10. Coordinating virtual staging and twilight requests

Hand off: Collecting the selected images and instructions, placing the approved request with your provider, tracking its return and routing it for review.

Done looks like: The returned work matches the request and is clearly identified for the person approving delivery.

You keep: Approval of the treatment, client expectations and any disclosure requirements. Coordinating the request is not the same as creating or professionally judging the edit.

11. Managing tour and floor-plan correction requests

Hand off: Submitting the specific corrections needed for Matterport tours or floor plans through your existing provider, then confirming the revised deliverable has arrived.

Done looks like: The request names the issue, the updated version is linked to the right job and the relevant reviewer has checked it.

You keep: Technical questions and final approval where specialist knowledge is required. Your coordinator should know when to escalate rather than guess.

12. Sending routine client updates

Hand off: Acknowledging requests, sharing an approved status update, clarifying missing information and letting the client know the next step. Give the person access to the actual project status so they can answer accurately.

Done looks like: The client receives a useful answer, the conversation is recorded and any commitment has an owner.

You keep: Sensitive complaints, refunds, pricing changes and promises outside the agreed service. Never ask someone to reassure a client with a deadline the team cannot support.

13. Keeping the CRM organized

Hand off: Updating contact details, categorizing records correctly and recording the next action from a real conversation. This should make the CRM reflect what actually happened.

Done looks like: Another team member can open the record and understand who the client is, what they need and who owes them a response.

You keep: The rules for stages, ownership and outreach eligibility. A tidy contact list is not permission to contact someone who opted out.

14. Tracking reshoots and service mistakes through resolution

Hand off: Logging the issue, assigning the next step and checking that the corrective work reaches the client. Use your existing project tracker; the original role description uses Monday.com.

Done looks like: The problem has a named owner, a resolution and a record of what changed. "We told someone about it" is not closed.

You keep: Decisions about compensation, serious quality failures and changes to the process that caused the issue.

15. Helping clients book appointments

Hand off: Booking within the approved availability and service rules in your existing scheduler, confirming the details and identifying missing information before the appointment.

Done looks like: The correct service, location, contact and appointment details are recorded, with any exception escalated.

You keep: Capacity decisions, special travel arrangements and commitments outside normal availability. The coordinator should not have to invent the rules while speaking with the client.

Which three should you hand off first?

Look at your last five completed shoots. Write down every time you had to check a status, forward a message, chase a file or repeat an instruction.

Choose three tasks that meet these conditions:

  • They happen often enough to justify teaching them.
  • You can show someone what a good result looks like.
  • A mistake can be caught in review before it reaches the client.
  • The person has the access, information and authority to finish the task.

A sensible starting point might be the master shoot list, deliverable tracking and routine status updates. An agency with a heavy editing queue may get more use from revision tracking first. The right order depends on where your work stalls.

Give the person a handoff, not just a task title

For each responsibility, write down six things: what starts the task, what information they need, the steps, the standard for completion, the deadline and when to escalate.

For example, "handle photo revisions" becomes:

After the morning review, send the editor one numbered list of corrections with the image names and deadline. Check the returned files against that list. If a requested change is unclear or the deadline is at risk, bring it to the project lead before telling the client it is resolved.

Start with a few jobs under supervision. Note what the person can complete independently, what still comes back to you and which instruction needs to be clearer. Measure your actual review time before assuming how much time the handoff will save.

Build the role around the work you actually have

These 15 responsibilities describe several kinds of work: project coordination, client communication, administration and creative production. One person may cover a useful combination. That does not mean every project manager should be your best editor, designer and client closer too.

Start with the recurring workload, define the approvals and hire for the skills the role actually needs. You may find that a better process is enough, that a part-time coordinator fits, or that a dedicated role makes sense.
