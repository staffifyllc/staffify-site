How Much Does a Video Editor Cost? Freelance vs Dedicated vs Agency

The real math on freelance rates, dedicated hires, and agency retainers. Plus the volume threshold where each option stops making sense.

Staffify Team · July 27, 2026 · 6 min read

If you're publishing more than a couple of videos a month, the question stops being should I hire a video editor and starts being which model actually costs less at my volume. The three options look similar on the surface. They price very differently once you run the numbers past 8 videos a month.

Here's the honest breakdown. No hand-waving, no hidden fees glossed over. Just what each option costs, what you get, and where the break-even points sit.

The Three Models, Priced Honestly

Every video editor you can hire falls into one of three buckets. Freelance (per video or per hour), dedicated (flat monthly rate for one person working just for you), or agency (a team assigned to your account with project management wrapped in).

The trap is comparing sticker prices. A $75 freelance edit and a $2,000 monthly dedicated editor look wildly different until you multiply the freelancer by your monthly volume. Then the math flips.

Freelance Video Editor Pricing

On Upwork and Fiverr, US-based freelance editors typically charge $50 to $150 per short-form video (under 90 seconds) and $200 to $600 for long-form (5 to 20 minutes). Offshore rates run 40 to 60 percent lower but come with revision cycles that can eat the savings.

Hourly, expect $35 to $85 for solid mid-market editors and $100+ for editors who can handle motion graphics, color grading, and sound design at a broadcast level.

What freelance pricing doesn't include:

  • Time you spend sourcing, testing, and re-sourcing when they ghost
  • Onboarding to your brand style every project (or paying for a style guide they may or may not follow)
  • Rush fees, which run 25 to 50 percent extra
  • Revision limits (most cap at 2 rounds, then charge per additional round)

Dedicated Video Editor Cost

A dedicated video editor is a full-time person working only on your content. In the US, that's a $65K to $95K salary plus benefits and payroll tax, so roughly $6,500 to $9,000 per month all-in.

Through a staffing partner using vetted global talent, a dedicated editor lands between $1,800 and $3,200 per month, flat. Same person every day. Full-time hours. No per-video math.

You're paying for capacity, not output. If you feed them 4 videos, that's expensive per video. If you feed them 20, it's cheap per video. This is why the model rewards volume.

Agency Video Editing Service Cost

Agencies typically charge $2,500 to $8,000 per month for a package of 8 to 20 videos, depending on complexity. The markup covers project management, backup editors, and their own margin (usually 40 to 60 percent above what they pay the actual editor).

The upside: you don't manage anyone. The downside: you're rarely working with the same editor twice, and turnaround is bound by their queue, not your priorities.

The Math at Different Volumes

Let's price out three realistic scenarios for a service business producing short-form social content (say, 60 to 90 second clips with captions, B-roll, and light motion graphics). Assume $85 per video for a decent US freelancer.

4 Videos Per Month

  • Freelance: $340
  • Dedicated (staffed): $2,400
  • Agency: $2,500 minimum retainer

At this volume, freelance wins by a mile. You don't have enough work to justify a dedicated seat. Stick with a freelancer you like and pay per video.

8 Videos Per Month

  • Freelance: $680
  • Dedicated (staffed): $2,400
  • Agency: $2,800 to $3,500

Still cheaper on paper to go freelance. But this is where the operational cost starts to matter. Managing 8 briefs, 8 revision cycles, and 8 file transfers a month is a real time sink. If your time is worth $100+ an hour, the freelance model is quietly costing you another $800 to $1,200 in owner-time each month.

16 Videos Per Month

  • Freelance: $1,360, plus 10 to 15 hours of your time managing it
  • Dedicated (staffed): $2,400, plus about 2 hours of your time
  • Agency: $4,500 to $6,500

Dedicated wins. You're paying $1,040 more than freelance in cash but saving 8 to 13 hours of owner time. And the output quality gets better every month because one person is learning your brand, your audience, and your editing preferences.

25+ Videos Per Month

  • Freelance: $2,125+ and it's now a part-time job for you
  • Dedicated (staffed): $2,400 to $3,200 (one editor can handle this range)
  • Agency: $7,000 to $10,000

Not close. A dedicated editor at this volume costs about $100 to $128 per video. Agencies are triple that. Freelance saves cash only if your time is free (it isn't).

Where the Break-Even Actually Sits

The cash break-even between freelance and dedicated lands right around 28 videos per month at typical freelance rates. The total cost break-even (cash plus your time) sits much lower, closer to 8 to 10 videos per month for most operators.

Put another way: if you're publishing at least two short-form videos a week, the dedicated model is almost certainly cheaper once you count the hours you're spending on vendor management.

The break-even between dedicated and agency almost never favors the agency unless you genuinely can't spare 15 minutes a day to direct one person. Agencies exist to solve a management problem, not a cost problem.

What Each Model Is Actually Good For

Freelance is best when:

  • You publish fewer than 6 videos a month
  • Your content is inconsistent (product launches, campaigns, one-offs)
  • You need specialty work occasionally (a sizzle reel, a case study video)
  • You have someone in-house who can brief and QC the output

Dedicated is best when:

  • You publish 8+ videos a month on a repeatable format
  • Brand consistency matters (same intro, same pacing, same style)
  • You want turnaround under 24 hours without paying rush fees
  • You're building a content engine, not filling gaps

Agency is best when:

  • You truly cannot dedicate any owner time to managing an editor
  • You need multiple specialties (editor, motion designer, colorist) but not full-time
  • You need a partner who will also do strategy, not just execution
  • Budget is not the constraint (it rarely isn't)

Hidden Costs Nobody Talks About

Sticker price is only half the picture. The costs that quietly wreck budgets:

Revision cycles. Freelancers usually cap at 2 rounds. Real-world video projects need 3 to 4. Every extra round is $30 to $75 or a favor you're burning.

Onboarding drag. A new freelancer takes 3 to 5 projects to internalize your style. If you're rotating freelancers because one keeps ghosting, you're paying for onboarding every quarter.

File and asset management. Who owns the project files? Where do brand assets live? Freelancers rarely maintain a clean library. Dedicated editors do because it's their job for the long haul.

Turnaround risk. A freelancer with three other clients will deprioritize you when someone else pays more. A dedicated editor's whole day is yours.

Quality drift. Agency work often starts strong and degrades as your account gets assigned to more junior editors over time. Ask any operator who's been on an agency retainer for 18 months.

How to Decide This Week

Run this quick check. Pull up your content calendar and count how many videos you actually shipped in the last 60 days. Not what you meant to ship. What went live.

Divide by two. That's your realistic monthly volume.

If it's under 6, stay freelance. Find one editor you like, pay them well, keep them close. Don't overcomplicate it.

If it's 8 to 20, run the dedicated math. A good staffed editor at $2,000 to $2,800 a month, working only on your content, will out-produce your current setup by month two. The cash cost is often within $500 of what you're already spending on freelance plus owner time.

If it's 20+, the only real question is whether you want one dedicated editor or two. Agencies at this volume are just an expensive tax on not wanting to send Slack messages.

The operators who get content right aren't the ones who found a magic vendor. They're the ones who matched their production model to their actual volume, and stopped trying to make a freelance setup do dedicated work.
