Customer Service SOPs: 9 Procedures and a Free Template

Build a customer service playbook with nine practical SOPs, a filled first-response example and a free downloadable template for ownership and escalation.

Staffify Team · August 26, 2026 · Updated September 22, 2026

A customer service SOP is a short set of instructions for a repeatable support task. It should tell the rep when to act, what they may decide, when to escalate, and how to confirm the work is complete. A useful playbook reduces guesswork without asking a rep to ignore context.

Use the one-page template · See a filled example · Download the editable template

A customer service SOP template

Copy these fields into your shared workspace. Assign an owner and review date before the team relies on it. The download includes the same fields and a sample first-response procedure. No email address is required.

  • Procedure and owner: name, version, approval date, and next review.
  • Trigger and scope: which requests, channels, and staffed hours it covers.
  • Inputs: required context, identity checks where needed, and approved tools.
  • Steps: numbered actions and the expected output of each.
  • Decision limits: what the rep may approve and what must be escalated.
  • Escalation: primary owner, backup, acknowledgment target, and handoff format.
  • Done: resolution evidence, record updates, and customer confirmation.
  • Review: sampled tickets, repeat-contact checks, and changes to the procedure.

Filled example: first response to a support email

Illustrative setup: a team staffed Monday–Friday, 9 a.m.–5 p.m. Eastern, chooses a two-business-hour first-response target. This is an example to adapt, not a Staffify service guarantee or universal benchmark.

  1. Check whether the request is already open, assign an owner, and identify the customer’s actual question.
  2. Classify the issue. Follow the relevant approved policy; flag urgent issues to the named escalation owner.
  3. If resolution needs more time, acknowledge the request and give a specific next update within the staffed schedule.
  4. Record the owner, next action, and update deadline in the ticket. Route exceptions with context.
  5. Confirm the resolution with the customer, update the record, and close according to the team’s policy.

Reply starting point: “Hi [name], I have your request about [issue]. I’m checking [next step] and will update you by [time and time zone]. Your reference is [ticket].” Only promise an update you can actually deliver.

Nine procedures to build into your support playbook

1. First response

Specify staffed hours, the response target, required acknowledgment, the next update time, and who takes over when the assigned rep is unavailable. Measure the target during staffed hours and define what happens outside them.

2. Refunds and credits

List the reasons, evidence, and approval limits for each action. Leave the amounts blank until the business owner approves them. A rep should never invent a refund policy from an example in an article.

3. Escalation

Name the next owner and backup, the trigger for escalation, and the expected acknowledgment time. Send a short summary, the customer’s request, what has been tried, and the decision needed.

4. Response templates

Build starting points for repeat issues from authorized, anonymized tickets. Require the rep to check facts, personalize the reply, and avoid promises outside the approved policy.

5. Exceptions

Log unusual requests, the decision maker, and the resolution. Review repeat exceptions and add them to the relevant procedure rather than relying on a private message history.

6. Complaints

Acknowledge the issue, confirm what the customer needs, and give a realistic next step. Define when to involve a manager and how to record the resolution. Do not promise an outcome before checking authority.

7. Delivery handoff

Send the delivery team the job identifier, expected versus actual result, deadline, and owner. Keep the ticket open until the support owner verifies the outcome and updates the customer.

8. Cancellations and retention

Respect a clear cancellation request and the applicable process. Where appropriate, ask for feedback and offer an approved alternative without obstructing cancellation. Record the request, effective date, and confirmation.

9. Weekly review

Review overdue tickets, repeat contacts, escalations, and sampled completed work. Update the procedure that caused the most avoidable confusion, record the change, and tell the team what changed.

Roll out the playbook on real examples

Start with your most frequent issue, first response, and escalation. Walk a rep through a sanitized past ticket, then have them handle a second example without help. Record where they need clarification and revise the instructions. Review live work before expanding decision authority.

Measure response time, overdue backlog, repeat contacts, resolution quality, and customer feedback. Compare similar ticket types and staffed hours; a faster acknowledgment alone does not prove better support.
